What it is

The shell that controls everything

A building envelope is the entire system of materials and design that controls the air movement, water-vapor movement and temperature of a structure — generally the exterior shell: walls, roof and floor. For a comfortable, stable indoor climate, the insulation and the moisture-and-air barrier have to work together. When they don't, you get drafts, condensation and high bills no matter how much insulation is stuffed in.

AIR

Air control

Foam self-seals the gaps and penetrations that let conditioned air leak out and outdoor air pour in.

MOISTURE

Vapor control

Closed-cell foam adds a moisture barrier where you need one — key in a cold climate where condensation causes damage.

TEMPERATURE

Thermal control

Continuous insulation with fewer gaps means fewer cold spots and a more even, easier-to-hold indoor temperature.

Why foam

Two jobs, one pass

Most assemblies use separate insulation and a separate air barrier — and the air barrier rarely gets sealed perfectly in the field. Spray foam insulates and air-seals at the same time, flashing itself around wire, pipe and duct penetrations. That's why a foamed envelope tends to actually perform the way the drawings intended, instead of leaking at every seam.

Questions

Common questions

What is the building envelope?

It's the entire system of materials that controls air movement, water-vapor movement and temperature between inside and outside — the exterior shell: walls, roof and floor. When the insulation and the air/moisture barrier work together, the indoor climate stays comfortable and stable.

Why does spray foam suit the envelope so well?

Because it insulates and air-seals in the same pass. Most assemblies rely on separate insulation plus a separate air barrier that rarely get sealed perfectly; foam does both at once and self-flashes around penetrations, so the envelope actually performs the way it's supposed to.

Where does the envelope leak the most?

Usually the top (attic/roofline), the bottom (rim joists and crawlspace), and penetrations for wire, pipe and ducts. Those are the spots we target first because they drive the most air movement.
